# Types of Oral Corrective Feedback

Lyster and Ranta's (1997) six types, with examples for the learner error *"Yesterday I go to the park."*

| Type | What the teacher does | Example | Learner must... |
|------|----------------------|---------|-----------------|
| Explicit correction | Gives the correct form and signals the error | "Not *go* - *went*. Yesterday I went." | Repeat |
| Recast | Reformulates the utterance correctly, without signalling | "Oh, you went to the park yesterday?" | Notice (often does not) |
| Clarification request | Signals a problem with meaning or form | "Sorry?" / "Pardon?" | Reformulate |
| Metalinguistic feedback | Comment, information or question about the form | "Yesterday - past or present?" / "We need the past tense." | Self-correct |
| Elicitation | Prompts the learner to complete or reformulate | "Yesterday I...?" (pause) / "How do we say that in the past?" | Self-correct |
| Repetition | Repeats the error, usually with rising intonation | "Yesterday I *go*?" | Self-correct |

Prompts (clarification, metalinguistic, elicitation, repetition) push the learner to self-correct; recasts and
explicit correction give the answer.

## What the research suggests (summary)

- Recasts are the most frequent teacher move but often go unnoticed, especially for grammar and with lower-level
  learners: learners may hear them as agreement ("Yes, you went to the park").
- Prompts lead to more learner repair (uptake) and work well when learners have partly learned the form.
- Explicit correction and metalinguistic feedback help with new or complex forms.
- Feedback that leads to learner self-repair tends to be retained better.
- Pronunciation and lexical errors are often noticed in recasts; grammatical ones less so.
- No single type is best: match the type to the error, the learner and the lesson stage.
- Learners generally want correction; anxiety, age and culture affect how they react.

## Making recasts more noticeable

- Short, partial recasts focused on the error: "*Went.*"
- Stress the corrected part: "You WENT to the park."
- Rising intonation to make it a check, then wait.
- Follow with a prompt if there is no uptake: "Say it again?"

## Choosing a type

| Situation | Suggested type |
|-----------|----------------|
| Learner knows the form (slip) | Repetition, elicitation, a gesture |
| Form partly known, accuracy stage | Elicitation or metalinguistic clue |
| New or complex form | Explicit correction + brief explanation |
| Pronunciation | Model + choral and individual drill; recast with stress |
| Vocabulary gap during fluency work | Quick recast or supply the word; note for later |
| Meaning unclear | Clarification request |
| Fluency activity, non-blocking errors | Note and correct later (delayed) |

## Uptake and repair

- **Uptake**: the learner's response right after feedback.
- **Repair**: uptake that corrects the error.
- Give time (3-5 seconds) after a prompt; let peers help if the learner cannot; then give the form.
- Ask the learner to say the whole sentence once corrected.

## Non-verbal techniques

- Finger correction: one finger per word; point to the wrong word or missing word.
- Gestures for tense (thumb back over the shoulder = past), for contractions (pinch fingers), for word order (cross
  arms).
- Facial expression of "not quite" (a raised eyebrow) as a gentle signal.
